# Introduction — Sequence 1: Opening crawl

Production prompt package, version 1. English. Scope ends as the crawl disappears; Earth and the ship belong to Sequence 2.

## Recommended method

Create the background, voice, and music separately. Add the exact title and crawl as editable text in the video editor. Record Bee first, then time the crawl to the actual performance. This gives control over spelling, reading speed, and pauses.

A single high-resolution starfield image held beneath animated text is sufficient. Video generation is optional for this sequence. Use black generated inside the editor for the opening, then fade in the starfield.

Working format: 16:9, 1920 × 1080, 24 fps. These are starting choices, not a locked format for the whole film. Keep a high-resolution background if available.

## Prompt 1 — Starfield background

Use in an image generator, or in text-to-video for a short background plate:

> A restrained cinematic view of deep space, a nearly black field scattered with fine distant stars. Natural irregular star placement, mostly tiny dim white points with a few subtle cool-blue and warm-white stars. Spacious, quiet, welcoming, with a sense of immense distance. The central region is sparsely populated with stars, leaving generous dark space for lettering to be added later. Uniform deep-black background, clean fine detail, understated brightness. Empty space and stars only. Locked-off composition, fixed viewpoint, stable star positions, a single uninterrupted shot. Widescreen 16:9.

If the tool provides a separate negative-prompt field:

> text, lettering, logos, watermark, planets, moons, spacecraft, people, faces, bright nebulae, lens flare, star trails, hyperspace, camera shake, zoom, cuts, flashing stars

For an image-to-video tool, attach the approved starfield and use this shorter motion prompt:

> Locked-off camera. Hold this exact starfield composition. The distant stars remain steady in position and brightness throughout one continuous shot. Quiet, still deep space.

Generate one short test at a supported duration. If the result drifts or flickers, use the approved still for the entire sequence. Fades and title motion will supply movement. Do not generate many consecutive starfield clips to cover the narration.

## Prompt 2 — Bee's voice direction

Use as voice direction, separately from the spoken script:

> A young boy telling a listener about the world he calls home. Clear English, warm, thoughtful, quietly curious, conversational and sincere. An ordinary child's speaking voice with natural variation and small pauses. Unhurried but engaged; approximately 115–125 words per minute as an initial guide. Begin with one small, natural intake of breath. The history lesson carries mild puzzlement. Everyday life on Earth feels familiar and secure. Space travel brings gentle wonder. “Then their messages stopped” becomes quieter, without theatrical menace. Give “There were friends on that ship” a simple, personal weight. End “Now Second Horizon is going to look for them” with calm purpose and hope. Dry isolated speech, one speaker, natural breaths. Keep narration separate from music and sound effects. Read the supplied script exactly.

Use an available suitable voice or an authorised performer. Save the selected voice and settings as Bee's reference for Departure, the flashback, and Back to Earth. The audio file may be labelled Bee; his name is not spoken or shown in this opening. If voice direction is unsupported, apply it through voice selection and performance rather than pasting it into the spoken-text field.

Pronunciation starting choice: First Horizon / Second Horizon as ordinary words; XYZ as “ex why zed.” Keep that choice consistent in later dialogue.

## Exact narration and crawl text

On Earth, we stopped using money so long ago that most of us have forgotten it.

I saw some once, in a history lesson. Little metal circles. Pieces of paper. Numbers on a screen. It was strange to think you might need those things before someone would give you food.

Now we grow things, build things, and share what we know. We look after each other because we care. There are no wars anymore.

We still have things to learn. Sometimes it takes a lot of talking to understand one another.

Our cities float on the sea. Our ships travel between planets. Humans, robots, androids, and cyborgs live and explore together. There are so many ways to be someone.

Years ago, a ship called First Horizon reached a planet we call XYZ. Its crew sent word of breathable air, oceans, and two moons.

Then their messages stopped. The ship disappeared.

There were friends on that ship.

Now Second Horizon is going to look for them.

## Prompt 3 — Original instrumental music

> Original instrumental opening cue for a hopeful science-fiction film about a peaceful Earth and a journey to meet another civilisation. Warm sustained strings, a few delicate celesta notes, soft woodwind colour, restrained low orchestral support. Begin almost silently and open gently as stars and the title appear. Leave a spacious, unobtrusive middle for a child's narration. Introduce a slight unresolved harmonic change as the missing expedition is mentioned, then return to tender hope. An intimate sense of discovery within a vast universe. A gradual soft ending with room for the final words to disappear. Instrumental throughout, a fresh original melody, understated dynamics, gentle rhythmic movement. Approximately two minutes, with an extendable quiet middle and a clean ending.

Use the approved narration length to determine the final cue duration. The musical target is warmth and discovery; the visual crawl reference does not prescribe the Star Wars melody or fanfare.

## Text-animation brief — For the editor or motion-graphics tool

Create this with editable text rather than generated lettering:

1. Hold pure black for about two seconds. Hear Bee's small intake of breath near the end.
2. Fade in the starfield over about two seconds as the music begins to swell.
3. Display HAPPY BEINGS, with EPISODE IV — NEW HORIZONS beneath it. Use clean, bold, highly legible lettering in warm pale gold. Hold for roughly three seconds, then recede smoothly towards an upper-centre vanishing point. Avoid copying the Star Wars logo treatment.
4. Introduce the first crawl paragraph as Bee begins speaking. Use the exact narration text above, preserving the nine paragraph breaks and normal sentence case. Do not include character cues or END CRAWL on screen.
5. Set the crawl on a plane tilted away from the viewer, rising from the lower frame towards the same vanishing point. Begin with the text column around two-thirds of the frame width. Use generous line spacing and conspicuous gaps between paragraphs.
6. Make each paragraph readable in the lower-to-middle portion of the image when its narration starts. Keep it comfortably readable through its spoken ending. Adjust line breaks, paragraph spacing, and smooth motion to the recording. Never shrink a long paragraph merely to meet an arbitrary speed. Avoid abrupt stops or velocity changes.
7. After the final spoken sentence, allow approximately five to eight seconds for its paragraph to recede and fade into the starfield. Soften the music. End on stars for the next sequence's transition.

The narration is 165 words: expect roughly 80–86 seconds of speech before additional pauses. With the title and ending, plan approximately 1:40–2:00 initially. Final timing must follow the actual voice and a readability check, not this estimate.

## Review before approving

- Exact wording and spelling, with no duplicate or missing sentences.
- Bee sounds natural and remains unseen; no name or identity disclosure.
- Text is readable at ordinary viewing size, especially the longer paragraphs.
- Starfield remains stable, with no seams, flashes, or distracting objects.
- Music supports every word; speech remains clearly intelligible.
- Save clean background, voice, music, and editable text separately for revisions and Russian adaptation.
