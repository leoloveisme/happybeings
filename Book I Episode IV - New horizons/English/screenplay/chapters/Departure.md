# Departure

## Sequence 1 — A meaningful encounter

INT. HOUSE OF MEANINGFUL ENCOUNTERS — MORNING

CHEN WOLFGANG-HANS WEIZU-BREZZHIKOVSKI sits beside an open window. Beyond him,
sunlight moves across water.

The interviewer remains outside the frame. Leave room for Chen to consider each
answer.

**INTERVIEWER (O.S.)** Would you tell me your name?

**CHEN** Chen Wolfgang-Hans Weizu-Brezzhikovski.

**INTERVIEWER (O.S.)** And how do you describe yourself?

**CHEN** I am a thinker. I was designed before I was born, but I was naturally
born. Those things are not opposites. My parents hoped to give me certain
possibilities. I have spent my life deciding which of them are mine.

**INTERVIEWER (O.S.)** What do you choose now?

**CHEN** This journey.

**INTERVIEWER (O.S.)** Why?

**CHEN** Because a crew disappeared. Because robots, androids, cyborgs, and
natural beings are all beings. Because no conscious being should become a
question lost in distance just because reaching them is difficult.

A moment. Laughter reaches the window from below.

**INTERVIEWER (O.S.)** What do you hope to find?

**CHEN** The truth. And a way to meet it without taking anything from it.

## Sequence 2 — The city waking

EXT. HOUSE OF MEANINGFUL ENCOUNTERS — BALCONY — MORNING

Chen steps outside.

The city opens in broad circles across the water: terraced gardens, pale
buildings, quiet transit lines connecting floating districts.

Through the open doors of a common house, people share a workbench. Nearby,
someone passes a bowl of fruit around a circle of neighbours.

In the square, children practise choreoyoga. An elder on a balcony copies their
movements, extravagantly wrong, doing that on purpose. The children collapse
into laughter.

At the harbour waits SECOND HORIZON, white and still above the water.

OLGA AHLAM PELIN-LURIE joins Chen. Navigation lights pulse beneath the
transparent skin of her wrist.

**OLGA** You are thinking yourself into another century.

**CHEN** Only a few days.

**OLGA** For you, perhaps. For the ship, the route has already begun.

Chen watches the waiting ship.

**CHEN** Do you think they are alive?

**OLGA** The first crew?

**CHEN** All of them.

Olga rests her hands on the railing.

**OLGA** I think we owe them an answer.

They remain together for a moment, looking towards the harbour.

## Sequence 3 — Something warm to give away

PEIJIN clears her throat from the doorway. A travel bag hangs over her
shoulder; her pale hair catches the light.

**PEIJIN** If you two are going to become statues, please do it after the
departure celebration. Nick has repaired the welcome fountain three times, and
he is becoming emotionally attached to it.

**NICK (O.S.)** It was not broken. It was expressing a structural preference.

Peijin laughs.

**PEIJIN** You hear that? The fountain has a preference. We are invited to
respect it.

NICK appears from the path below, carrying an engineering case and a folded
blanket the colour of the sea before sunrise. His beard is braided for travel.

Olga indicates the blanket.

**OLGA** What is that for?

**NICK** XYZ may have weather.

**PEIJIN** Most planets do.

**NICK** If someone is cold, it seems sensible to bring something warm that can
be given away.

Chen touches the edge of the woven fabric.

**CHEN** That is sensible.

Nick brightens. Together, they head towards the music at the harbour.

## Sequence 4 — Still saying goodbye

EXT. HARBOUR — MORNING

Fruit, music, conversation. Friends embrace beside the boarding ramp. Natural
beings, androids, robots, and cyborgs mingle among the travellers and their
families.

QUIRONG waits at the foot of the ramp, its dark composite body turned towards
the gathering.

**QUIRONG** Crew present. Departure readiness: ninety-six per cent.

**PEIJIN** What is the missing four?

**QUIRONG** You are all still saying goodbye.

**NICK** That is not a fault.

**QUIRONG** I did not say it was.

Chen pauses beside Quirong as the others greet their friends.

**CHEN** You knew some of the first crew.

**QUIRONG** We worked together. One helped shape my early language model.

**CHEN** Are you afraid?

**QUIRONG** I have calculated several undesirable outcomes.

**CHEN** That was not my question.

Quirong turns towards him.

**QUIRONG** Then yes. I believe I am.

Peijin, still nearby, rests a hand lightly against its arm.

**PEIJIN** Thank you for saying so.

## Sequence 5 — Earth has relatives

Chen's nieces gather around him. All of them are present. Stay with their brief
farewell long enough for each niece to share a look, a touch, or an embrace
with her uncle.

For a moment he is simply theirs. He listens, smiling, as their goodbyes
overlap. The last embrace takes a little longer.

<!-- Casting/development note: include every one of Chen's nieces, even though their appearances are brief. Their names, ages, and number remain to be established. -->

As they rejoin the gathering, Chen opens his hand. A small pebble rests in his
palm.

Bee's voice is heard over this image, like a remembered request. Do not show
its speaker or identify his relationship to Chen. Chen listens without
answering aloud.

**BEE (V.O.)** Bring me pebbles from every planet you step on.

Chen turns the stone gently between his fingers.

**BEE (V.O.)** So Earth remembers it has relatives.

He closes his hand around it and slips it into his pocket.

<!-- Character note for the screenplay only: Bee is Bijaksana Tram Tu, Chen's nephew, also called Tram. His self-introduction and on-screen reveal are reserved for later. -->

## Sequence 6 — Departure

At the ramp, Olga waits for Chen. Beyond her, the ship's open doorway frames
Peijin, Nick, and Quirong.

**CHEN** We offer help. We leave if asked.

Olga nods. Chen takes one last look back at his family and boards.

The doorway closes. The music at the harbour comes to a gentle end. Faces turn
upwards.

Second Horizon rises from the water on a column of light.

Chen's nieces stand together among those waving from the harbour.

INT. SECOND HORIZON — VIEWING AREA

Gardens, bridges, and the gathered families grow small below. Chen keeps one
hand in the pocket holding the pebble.

Peijin stands beside him at the viewing glass.

**PEIJIN** What do you think we will find?

Chen watches Earth curve away, blue and beloved.

**CHEN** I do not know.

Olga looks into the vastness ahead.

**OLGA** Then we are ready to begin.

EXT. SPACE

Second Horizon travels out into the starfield.

CONTINUE INTO: VAST VAST SPACE.

Earth had forgotten money so completely that Chen sometimes wondered whether


the old word had ever belonged to a real language.



It survived in the House of Knowledge, carefully preserved in a sealed archive


no one needed to open. Children were shown images of metal discs, paper slips,


and numbers being moved between accounts. They found the whole arrangement


strange.



“Why would somebody need a symbol before helping another person?” a child had


once asked Chen.



The teachers had answered gently. The symbol had not been the reason people


helped; it had become the reason some people could not. Then they had moved on


to another lesson.



Chen was thinking of that morning when he stood at the outer balcony of the


House of meaningful encounters and watched the city below wake into light.



The city rose in broad circles from the water: gardens on terraces, pale


buildings opened to the sea air, quiet transit lines tracing shining curves


between the floating districts. At the centre of every circle stood a common


house—part workshop, part school, part place of celebration—where anyone could


bring an idea, a problem, a question, or simply themselves.



There were no markets. No advertisements. No locked doors.



There were still misunderstandings. There were still people who withdrew when


they were hurt, or spoke too quickly when they were afraid. There were still


meetings that lasted longer than anyone wanted because no one could yet feel


what the right answer was. Earth had become peaceful, generous, and free. It


had not become complete.



Below him, children were practising choreoyoga in a sunlit square. Their


movements were uncoordinated and delighted. An elder copied them from a


balcony, making every gesture extravagantly wrong until the children collapsed


into laughter.



“You are thinking yourself into another century.”



Olga Ahlam Pelin-Lurie joined him at the railing. Her travel coat was


silver-grey, plain except for the thin navigation lights beneath the


transparent skin of her wrist. They pulsed quietly, receiving and arranging


distances that were too large for an ordinary mind to hold.



“Only a few days,” Chen said.



“For you, perhaps.” Olga looked toward the harbour. “For the ship, the route


has already begun.”



That was how Olga spoke when she was content: as if space itself had joined the


conversation.



At the edge of the city waited *Second Horizon*, white and still above the


water. Its first engines had been prepared before sunrise. Its name had been


chosen without ceremony, because everyone understood what it meant. Years


earlier, *First Horizon* had been sent to a new world catalogued as XYZ.



The first report had been extraordinary.



Breathable air. Gravity close to Earth’s. Forests, oceans, weather patterns.


Two moons: one blue, one orange. No visible ruins. No signs of catastrophe.



Then the messages stopped.



Not gradually. Not with a distress call. One day, to be precise the day of


their arrival, the expedition was answering; the next, every robot, android,


cyborg, and system aboard was silent.



Earth had sent patient signals. It had sent repair instructions, greetings,


questions, invitations to respond in any form. Nothing returned.




But the ship had not vanished.

The observatories could still see its energy signature orbiting XYZ. It was
stable. Its systems did not look damaged. They looked—though no scientist
enjoyed using the word—asleep.


The ship had vanished completely. Earth’s observatories could find no wreckage,


no active energy signature, and no answer to their calls. Its disappearance was


the reason *Second Horizon* had been sent.



Much later, the crew would learn that *First Horizon* had not been destroyed.


The collective mind of XYZ had hidden it.



“Do you think they are alive?” Chen asked.



Olga did not pretend not to know who he meant. “The first crew?”



“All of them.”



She watched the ship in the harbour. “I think that unanswered questions are


somewhere. And I think we owe them an answer.”



Chen nodded. The first expedition had never been understood as disposable


equipment. Its robots and androids had names, preferences, memories, and chosen


work. The cyborgs who had travelled with them were people. If they could be


reached, Earth had a responsibility to reach them.



Behind them, someone cleared her throat with exaggerated patience.

“If you two are going to become statues,” she said, “please do it after the

“It was not broken,” Nick called from the path below. “It was expressing a


structural preference.”

Olga pointed at the blanket. “What is that for?”



“XYZ may have weather,” Nick said.



“Most planets do,” Peijin replied.



“We know nothing about the people there.” Nick folded the fabric once more,

can be given away.”



Chen looked at the blanket. It was a simple object, hand-woven by someone who

Chen closed his hand around the pebble. For a moment he saw the mission as an


old kind of journey: a distant planet, an unknown people, a ship sent to find


out what was there. Then he thought of the first expedition’s silent orbit and

XYZ was not an empty question waiting for Earth to answer. It was already


somebody’s home.



The crew had been instructed to observe with restraint. To land only if it was


safe. To offer help. To leave if asked.



It was a good instruction. Chen hoped it was good enough.



As they boarded, the city became quiet—not with fear, but with attention.

and dancing children grew small beneath them. Earth curved away, blue and


beloved.

He thought of friends asleep in the dark. Of an entire planet watched from too


far away. Of the thin line between curiosity and intrusion, and how easily good


intentions could cross it without noticing.



“I do not know,” he said.



Olga looked into the vastness ahead.



“Then we are ready to begin.”
