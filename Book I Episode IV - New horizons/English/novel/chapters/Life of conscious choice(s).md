An Var took them through the forest by a path that did not appear until she
stepped toward it.

The Earth crew followed her between pale trees whose branches held small golden
fruit. Moss grew in blue cushions around the roots. Insects made a many-layered
clicking music beneath the leaves, and the wind carried a deep, slow note
through the trunks. Nick tried to decide whether it was music or weather.
Before he decided, a bird answered it with three sharp calls and the entire
forest changed rhythm.

“There is always something going on,” An Var said. “It depends where you put
your attention.”

Peijin stopped beside a cluster of white mushrooms. Her hand hovered above
them. “They are singing.”

An Var’s amber eyes brightened. “Yes.”

“Very quietly.”

“They have no reason to be loud. They have all the time they need.”

Nick listened again. The mushrooms did not sound like machines, or animals, or
anything Earth’s instruments would have chosen to name. Yet they were doing
something together. The more attention he gave them, the more distinct their
small song became.

“Everything is communicating?” he asked.

“Everything,” An Var said. “Trees, mushrooms, birds, bugs, stones, water. You
call some things silent because you do not yet know how they speak.”

She led them to a lake hidden inside a bowl of smooth silver-grey rock. It was
perfectly still. The blue moon lay in its surface even though the sun had not
fully set. No building stood there. No machine, cable, pipe, or device
explained the clarity of the water.

Nick walked to the edge and looked down. He saw his own reflection. Beneath it,
he saw roots travelling through the ground, fish moving under distant ice, and
a line of migrating birds crossing a sky that was not the sky above him.

He stepped back. “I did not ask to see that.”

The images vanished.

An Var placed both bare feet on the stone. “The lake offered because you were
looking. It should have waited. I am sorry.”

“The lake can decide?” Nick asked.

“Everything can decide.”

The answer did not satisfy the engineer in him. But the care in An Var’s
apology did something stranger: it made him feel that the lake had heard the
correction too.

“All our experiences enter the conscious energy space,” An Var said. “Nothing
needs to be stored in a thing. When someone wants to understand, they ask. When
they are ready, knowledge is there.”

“Who maintains it?” Nick asked.

“Everyone.”

“That is not an answer.”

An Var smiled. “It is the answer.”

Olga sat at the water’s edge. “On Earth, we share knowledge too. We have
houses, records, devices, and people who care for them.”

“That is beautiful,” An Var said. “You have made so many ways to help one
another remember.”

The lake stirred.

At first the crew assumed a fish had broken the surface. Then the water rose in
a narrow, shining column. A figure appeared upside down beneath it, as though
the bottom of the lake had become the sky and the shore had become its floor.

She fell upward.

The figure burst from the water, turned once in the air, and landed lightly on
the lake shore a few steps from them. Droplets remained around her for a
moment, floating like small moons before falling back into the lake.

She was laughing.

Her chosen form was tall and fluid, green-gold at the throat and shoulders,
with dark hair streaming wet down her back. She looked from the lake to An Var,
then to the visitors. Her gaze stopped on Quirong.

Quirong had moved before anyone else did. It had stepped forward with both
hands raised, ready to catch her, although she had plainly not needed catching.

The being’s laughter softened. “You thought I was falling.”

“I did,” Quirong said.

“I was.” She looked back at the lake. “Just not in the direction you expected.”

For the first time since leaving Earth, Quirong seemed to have no prepared
response. It lowered its hands slowly. The silence between them did not feel
empty. It felt newly made.

An Var placed one hand against the stranger’s shoulder. “This is Ya Sha.”

Ya Sha’s attention returned to the crew, but it returned to Quirong once more
before she spoke. “And you are the being who came to find the ones who
disappeared.”

“I am Quirong,” it said.

“I am happy you came, Quirong.”

The words were simple. Quirong stored them with a care none of the others could
see.

Ya Sha sat beside the lake and began squeezing water from her hair. “An Var
told me you are learning about choosing.”

“We are trying,” Chen said.

“That is a good beginning.” Ya Sha looked at Nick. “Do you enjoy being you?”

The question was direct but not demanding. Nick thought of the names people had
given him, of the questions that had treated him as a puzzle, and of the time
it had taken to understand that no explanation was required for him to exist.

“Yes,” he said. “I enjoy being me as I am now.”

Peijin touched his arm. “I know you do.”

Nick smiled at her. “And you?”

“I enjoy being me,” Peijin said. “Not because I have never changed. Because I
have.”

Ya Sha’s face opened with delight. “Then you already know a lot about conscious
choice.”

She lifted one wet hand. The green-gold of her skin deepened to blue at the
fingertips, then returned to green. “Some of us change forms often. Some of us
choose one form for a whole life. Both are good. We can be trees, fish, birds,
bugs, air, water, mountains. We can be any moving and breathing being. Or we
can be exactly who we are for as long as we want.”
