The evening began with laughter from the hill above the sea.

Chen heard it before he saw anyone. It came in bright bursts across the warm
air, mingled with the breathing of the tide and a sound from the trees that was
almost singing. He and the others had accepted An Var’s invitation to walk the
shore.

“You will see,” she had said. “They are enjoying being.”

At the top of the hill, the Earth crew stopped. Below them, a broad beach
curved around a bay of darkening blue water. The orange moon was rising beyond
the far headland. The blue moon was already high, scattering a pale
green-silver path over the sea.

Col An and Sai Ya were running barefoot along the wet sand.

To Earth eyes, Col An’s chosen form might have looked human: a tall, lean young
man with dark close-cropped hair, strong shoulders, warm brown skin, and a
smile so immediate that it seemed to move toward the world. But it was only the
form he had chosen for that evening. Fine blue lines travelled beneath his skin
from throat to wrist, brightening whenever he laughed.

Sai Ya ran beside him, quick and sure-footed. Her body was strong and graceful;
her light hair lay loose around one shoulder. Dark leaf-like patterns curled
from her hands over her arms and along her side. They were not markings placed
upon her, Chen understood, but part of the living surface of the form she had
chosen. Her pale eyes caught the blue moon and seemed to hold it.

Neither of them wore clothes.

For a moment, Nick looked away. Peijin did not. She watched with the still,
attentive expression she used when another being was trying to describe a
feeling for which they had no words.

“Are they performing?” Nick asked quietly.

An Var laughed. “No. Why would they perform?”

Nick considered that. “I do not know.”

“They are playing,” An Var said. “They have finished the times of watching and
trying. They are in the time of being and enjoyment.”

Col An and Sai Ya had each spent two hundred years observing the world, then
two hundred years experimenting with it. They were young adults now, within the
long season of life when no one expected them to become anything else before
they were ready.

Col An reached Sai Ya first and spun her gently by both hands. She escaped,
running backward, laughing, and he followed. Then they met again in the shallow
water, embraced, and kissed as naturally as they had run.

Chen felt an old Earth reflex rise in him: the feeling that he had accidentally
seen something private. It was not disapproval. It was uncertainty. He did not
know where to place his eyes or his hands.

An Var noticed. “You can look away if you want to,” she said. “They will not be
sad. But you do not have to look away because they are happy.”

“I do not think happiness is the problem,” Chen said.

“What is the problem?”

He did not answer at once. Below, Sai Ya pushed a wave toward Col An with both
hands. The wave rose higher than it should have, curved around him like a clear
wall, and broke over his head. His delighted shout carried up the hill.

“We are not used to seeing people without a boundary around them,” Chen said at
last.

“Clothes can be a nice boundary,” An Var said. “So can distance. So can saying
no. But being naked is not the same as asking to be taken from.”

Peijin nodded slowly. “That is true on Earth, too. We do not always remember
it.”

Farther along the beach, other XYZ beings had begun to gather. Some had skin
the deep blue of the higher moon. Some glowed orange and yellow at their hands,
cheeks, or shoulders. One being’s hair changed from green to copper as they
walked. Two smaller beings had four arms and were using all of them to throw
flat stones over the water. The shore had no audience and no performers. It had
only participants.

“The moons change,” Olga said. “Does that happen often?”

“They dance,” An Var replied. “Sometimes the blue moon becomes green or brown.
Sometimes the orange moon becomes yellow or red. They have their own rhythms.
And they listen to us, too.”

“They listen to you?” Nick asked.

“Everything listens,” An Var said.

As if answering her, the orange moon deepened toward red. A cheer rose from the
beach. Sai Ya lifted both arms, and the blue lines in Col An’s skin shifted to
orange at his wrists. Sai Ya’s leaf-patterns brightened gold. Around them,
bodies answered in blue and red, yellow and green, orange and blue. The whole
shore became part of the sky’s changing colour.

Col An saw An Var and waved. Then he saw the Earth crew. He did not stop or
cover himself. He simply smiled and opened both arms in greeting.

“Come,” An Var translated, though Col An had not spoken aloud. “He says: you
are welcome to enjoy this evening with us.”

Nick looked at Chen. Chen looked at Peijin, who was already walking down the
path toward the sand.

“I have spent my whole life helping beings feel safe in their bodies,” she said
over her shoulder. “I would like to learn what it is like when safety is the
beginning, not the destination.”

Olga followed her. Nick held back a moment longer, then set his shoes beside a
smooth black stone and stepped barefoot onto the beach.

Chen was last. The water reached his ankles, warm and insistent. Col An ran
past him, laughing at something Sai Ya had done, then returned to offer Chen a
hand—not as an instruction, not as a test, but as an uncomplicated invitation.

Chen took it.

The evening did not become a lesson. No one explained the right way to stand,
to swim, to dance, or to feel. Col An and Sai Ya ran, kissed, embraced, and ran
again. Later they went together into the sea, still laughing, and the others
gave them the space they wanted.

Around the fireless shore, music rose from clapping hands, humming voices, feet
in sand, and the patient water. The Earth crew joined where they could. They
were clumsy at first. Then less clumsy. Then, for a few minutes at least, they
forgot to ask whether they were doing it correctly.

Above them, the two moons went on dancing.
