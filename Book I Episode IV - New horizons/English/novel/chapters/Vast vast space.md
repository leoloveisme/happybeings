# Vast vast space

## Record of meaningful encounter: Olga Ahlam Pelin-Lurie

**Interviewer:** Would you tell me your name?

**Olga:** Olga Ahlam Pelin-Lurie.

**Interviewer:** And how do you describe yourself?

**Olga:** I am a navigator. I was created for long journeys and adapted for space
travel before I was awake enough to agree to anything. That used to sound sad
when other people said it. It does not always feel sad to me. I love space. I
love distance. I love finding a way through something so large that it does not
seem to have a way through it.

**Interviewer:** Did you choose this mission?

**Olga:** Yes. That is important. I was made with capacities. I choose what to do
with them.

**Interviewer:** What do you expect to find at XYZ?

**Olga:** A ship that is still there, and beings who may still be waiting. I hope
they know we have not forgotten them.

- - -
Space did not become empty simply because Earth had disappeared behind them. It
became full in a different way.

For twelve days, *Second Horizon* travelled through darkness freckled with
stars, through blue veils of gas and long regions where the instruments found
almost nothing at all. Chen spent hours at the viewing glass, looking for a
shape in the blackness. Peijin said space was teaching her patience. Nick said
it was teaching him how many things could go wrong without being visible.

Olga spent most of the journey in the navigation chamber. Light unfolded around
her: routes, gravitational currents, silent debris, and probabilities opening
and closing like flowers. She did not command the ship so much as join it in a
continuous act of attention.

When Chen found her there, XYZ was already visible in the field.

“There,” Olga said.

At first he saw only a point of colour. Then the field adjusted, and the planet
opened before him: deep blue-green seas, forests, pale mountain ranges, and
clouds moving over a world half in sunlight, half in night.

Around it moved two moons.

One was blue, clear and bright as paint in water. The other was a soft burning
orange.

“It looks like Earth,” Chen said.

“It looks like a planet,” Olga replied. “Earth does not own that shape.”

He laughed. “You are right. It is more colourful than Earth.”

“That is allowed.” replied Olga with a smirk. 

At the dining table that evening, nobody ate much. There was a sudden loss of
appetite, even though no one knew- why. 

“Do you think the people there are watching us?” Peijin asked.

Nick looked up from a diagnostic display. “If they have orbital monitoring,
then yes.”

“That is not what I meant.”

Nick lowered the display. “Yes. I think they might be.”

Quirong stood at the window, facing XYZ. “The first expedition will have been
visible from the surface as well,” it said.

The table went quiet.

On the day *First Horizon* arrived, it had transmitted one clear report: landing
successful; atmosphere breathable; gravity close to Earth’s; conditions
favourable for life. Then the signal stopped in the middle of an ordinary
transmission cycle.

No distress call followed. After the first message, every trace of *First
Horizon* disappeared from Earth’s instruments.

“We should have sent someone sooner,” Peijin said quietly.

“The journey takes time,” Chen replied.

“That is true. It does not make the waiting less real.”

Quirong turned from the window. “The first crew had agreed to autonomous
mission protocols. They understood that direct assistance could not arrive
quickly.”

“That does not mean they agreed to be abandoned,” Nick said.

“No,” Quirong said. “It does not.”

The next morning, *Second Horizon* entered XYZ’s outer orbital field. Olga
stood at navigation, Nick at engineering, and Quirong linked itself to the
passive sensors. Chen and Peijin watched the world turn below them.

They found no sign of *First Horizon*.

No broken orbit. No debris. No energy signature. No trace of its approach or
its departure. Its absence was so complete that Nick checked the ship’s sensors
three times before he allowed himself to say it aloud.

“It is hidden,” Olga said.

“Hidden by what?” Chen asked.

“I do not know.” She looked down at the blue-green world. “But something here
can make a ship disappear from every instrument we have.”

Below them, there were signs of habitation: open shapes among the trees, paths
curving toward the coast, and small lights gathered beside a river. The planet
was clearly not empty. It was somebody’s home.

Then a new signal appeared on the console—not an Earth language and not a code
from the first expedition. It was a warm, repeating pattern of tones, with a
map reference to an open stretch of shore.

Olga listened once. Her navigation lights shifted from white to soft blue.

“That,” she said, “looks like an invitation.”

Chen nodded. “We land there. Slowly. With no weapons, no drones, and no scans
we would not accept ourselves.”

Nick considered the shore, the signal, and Quirong’s still face. At last he
closed the engineering display.

“Agreed,” he said.

Below them, the orange moon began to rise over the sea.

*Second Horizon* turned toward the planet.
