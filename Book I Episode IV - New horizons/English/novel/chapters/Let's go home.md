# Let's go home

For the first three days after leaving XYZ, nobody asked what they should do when they reached Earth.

The question existed in every room of *Second Horizon*. It lived in the silence after meals, in the long hours at the viewing glass, in the way Nick checked systems that did not need checking and then stopped himself. It lived in Chen’s notebook, still unopened on the table beside his bed. It lived in Olga’s hands as they moved through the navigation field, guiding the ship along the long curve home.

But nobody made a plan.

They had gone to XYZ because a first expedition had disappeared. They had expected to return with an explanation, perhaps a repair, perhaps a rescue. Instead, they returned with awakened beings from the first ship, a two-hundred-year-old child from another world, and the knowledge that Earth’s kindest intentions could still cross a boundary without seeing it.

There was no argument about it.

There was grief. There was surprise. There was longing. There was also, beneath everything, a quiet gratitude that they had been allowed to learn.

An Var spent much of the journey near the viewing glass.

She did not need to sleep. The others did. In the night cycle, when the lights were lowered and the ship became soft with the breathing of its human passengers, she stood facing the stars. Sometimes Peijin found her there and brought a blanket that An Var did not need. An Var always accepted it anyway, wrapping it around her shoulders with grave delight.

“It is warm,” she said one night.

“That is what blankets are for.”

“I know. I like that you make things for warmth.”

Peijin stood beside her. “You do not have cold on XYZ?”

“We have cold. We change with it.”

“We change with it too. Just more slowly.”

An Var looked at her. “That is exciting.”

Peijin laughed quietly. “It is not always exciting.”

“No,” An Var said. “But it is alive.”

In the mornings, the crew gathered in the common room without calling it a meeting. The first expedition’s awakened travellers sometimes joined them. They were still remembering the long sleep in fragments: a sudden stillness, then gentle dreams full of colours they could not name, then voices inviting them back.

No one blamed XYZ.

At first, Chen had expected anger. He had prepared himself to defend the people of XYZ, though he was not certain he had the right. Instead, one of the androids from *First Horizon* said, “I would have objected to being put to sleep.” Then, after a long pause, it added, “I also object to what we did before that.”

Everyone understood.

Nick set down his cup. “We did not know.”

“No,” the android said. “We did not. But not knowing is not the same as not doing.”

An Var listened without interrupting. Later, she asked Nick why Earth beings kept saying *we did not know* as though it were both an explanation and an apology.

“Because sometimes it is both,” Nick said.

“And sometimes?”

“Sometimes it is only the beginning of an apology.”

An Var held that thought for a long time.

Olga continued to navigate. Her new friendship with An Var did not make her less precise; it made her precision gentler. She no longer spoke of the route as though it were something to conquer. She spoke of it as a relationship between the ship, the distance, and the people trusting her to bring them through it.

One afternoon, An Var sat beside her in the navigation chamber. The light of the route filled the room around them: gold lines, blue fields, small points that widened when Olga touched them.

“Does the ship choose?” An Var asked.

“Not in the way your lake chooses.”

“Does it want to arrive?”

Olga smiled. “I do not know.”

An Var’s eyes widened. “You do not know?”

“I am learning to say that.”

For a while they watched the course unfold.

“Were you sad when you left XYZ?” Olga asked.

“Yes.”

“Are you afraid of Earth?”

An Var considered the blue field of stars ahead. “A little.”

“You do not have to be brave all the time.”

“I know.” An Var leaned against Olga’s shoulder. “But I want to go.”

Chen finally opened his notebook on the sixth day.

He wrote only one sentence: *We must learn to ask before we look.*

Then he crossed it out.

The sentence was true, but it was too small. It made the lesson sound like a rule that could be followed and forgotten. He closed the notebook again.

Later, he found Nick in the observation room. Nick was not inspecting anything. He was watching the stars.

“You are changing,” Chen said.

“I am sitting down.”

“You know what I mean.”

Nick smiled without looking away. “I thought understanding a system meant being able to map it. XYZ has made that difficult.”

“Is that bad?”

“No.” Nick was quiet. “It is frightening. But not bad.”

Chen sat beside him. They watched the ship move through the dark.

“What will we tell people?” Nick asked.

Chen thought of the Houses of meaningful encounters, the open circles of Earth’s cities, the people who would want facts, methods, and assurances. He thought of An Var asking why they asked questions instead of knowing.

“The truth,” he said.

“Which truth?”

“That we met beings who were more developed than we were. That they are not better because they can do things we cannot. That we have a direction.”

Nick nodded slowly. “And that we need their permission before we call it a direction.”

“Yes.”

On the final evening before Earth appeared on the instruments, the crew gathered by the viewing glass. No ceremony had been planned. Someone had brought music, though no one played it. Someone else had brought food, though few of them ate.

An Var stood in the middle of them, holding the small pebble Chen had carried from Earth.

“Your nephew gave you this,” she said.

“He did.”

“He said Earth should remember it has relatives.”

Chen looked at the pebble in her hand. It was ordinary, grey, and smaller than a coin from the old archive. He had carried it across the distance between worlds and back again.

“Yes,” he said.

An Var closed her fingers around it. “Then Earth will remember.”

Beyond the glass, a familiar blue point began to grow.

No one said *home* at first.

Then Olga did.

“Let’s go home.”
